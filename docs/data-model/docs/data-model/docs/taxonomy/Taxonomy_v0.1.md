# Overview

The taxonomy provides a structured vocabulary for organizing archive material.

Its purpose is to improve discovery and comparison while preserving the original meaning and uncertainty of historical sources.

The taxonomy classifies information.

It does not determine explanations.

---

# Taxonomy Principles

## 1. Classification is Descriptive

Records are categorized according to what was reported, recorded, or documented.

Example:

A report describing unusual lights is classified as a light observation.

The classification does not assign an explanation.

---

## 2. Multiple Classifications Are Allowed

A single record may contain multiple classifications.

Example:

Historical maritime report:

- Source Type: Ship Log
- Phenomenon Type: Light Observation
- Geographic Setting: Coastal
- Historical Context: Navigation

---

## 3. Original Terminology Is Preserved

Historical terms, local names, and cultural descriptions remain available.

Modern classifications are added alongside original language.

---

# Record Type Classification

## Incident

A specific reported occurrence connected to a time and location.

## Tradition

A cultural narrative, belief, or story passed through communities.

## Observation

A recorded perception without a complete interpretation.

## Report

A documented account from a person, organization, or publication.

## Claim

A statement requiring additional evaluation.

## Interpretation

A proposed explanation of existing records.

## Hypothesis

A research idea generated from patterns within the archive.

---

# Taxonomy Evolution

The taxonomy will change over time.

New categories may be added when:

- new source types appear
- researchers identify useful distinctions
- additional cultures and languages are incorporated

Previous classifications remain preserved through version history.

---

# Taxonomy Principle

Categories should improve discovery while preserving complexity.

The taxonomy organizes information.

It does not reduce it.
